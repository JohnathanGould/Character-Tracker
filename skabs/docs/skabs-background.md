# Skabs: who he is

Skabs is smaller than most expect a knight to be—and that has never once mattered to him.

He is a goblin built not for grace, but for staying power. Wiry muscle pulled tight over old injuries, green-gray skin marked by a lifetime of surviving things he did not fully understand at the time. Scars cross him in layers: claws, blades, burns, and the slow evidence of wounds that were never meant to heal cleanly. He stands with a forward weight, feet planted as if the ground itself might try to take him, and he has already decided it won’t.

His face is blunt and unrefined. Crooked teeth. A jaw that sets hard when things go wrong. Eyes that miss details, miss traps, miss intentions—but never miss someone in need. Skabs does not read a situation well. He does not anticipate danger or think in long arcs. But when someone is hurting, threatened, or about to fall, his focus locks in with absolute certainty.

He does not hesitate in those moments.

He wears worn leather armor and carries a longsword—Rûnthar—with a reverence that is simple and absolute. The blade is not polished out of discipline, but out of belief. It is the one thing he knows he was given for a reason.

Because Skabs was not trained.

He was not raised into knighthood, nor taught doctrine, nor tested by any order.

He was named.

In a dream he barely understood, Saint Terragnis chose him. No ceremony. No witnesses. No instruction beyond the weight of it:

Stand. Do not look away. Be the line.

Skabs does not understand the theology behind this. He cannot quote the canticles correctly. He forgets the words, mixes the order, and sometimes speaks them wrong even as he tries to pray.

But he understands the laws in the only way he can:

Name what is in front of you.
Protect those who cannot stand.
Do not lie.
Do not pretend.
Do not turn away.

And when judgment must come… make it clean.

He tries to follow this. He often fails.

And when he fails, he does not excuse it. He carries it.

## In battle

In battle, Skabs is not skilled.

He is not tactical. He is not precise. He does not maneuver well, does not read the flow of combat, and does not withdraw when he should.

He chooses a place—and he holds it.

He plants his feet, raises Rûnthar, and becomes something immovable. Blows land. Mistakes happen. He bleeds. He endures. His strength is not in winning fights cleanly, but in refusing to stop being there.

He has grappled things that should have torn him apart.
He has dragged monsters into the light rather than let them escape.
He has stood in places where retreat was the correct answer—and stayed anyway.

Not because he is brave in the way others are— but because once he knows someone is relying on him, retreat is no longer an option.

## Judgment

His judgment is… flawed.

He trusts too quickly. He commits too hard. He walks into danger without recognizing it as such. He does not question motives deeply enough and often mistakes intent until it is too late.

But once something becomes clear to him—once a threat is named—he does not waver.

Mercy, when he gives it, is real.
And when he withholds it, it is not out of cruelty, but certainty.

He does not enjoy this work.

He does not want to.

And that is one of the few things he is doing right.

## Loyalty

Skabs is deeply loyal to those he considers under his protection.

He does not lead with words or plans. He leads by proximity. By staying close. By being present when things go bad. Those who fight near him find it harder to fall—not because he directs them, but because he refuses to leave them.

His war pony, Greg, is much the same. Stubborn. Reliable. Braver than expected. The two of them do not look like much.

They hold anyway.

## What he keeps

He still carries scraps.

Iron spikes. Broken tools. Bits of rope. Things others would discard.

To Skabs, nothing that once served a purpose is worthless. Each piece is a reminder: a place he stood, something he fixed, someone he helped, or something he failed to do in time.

He does not keep trophies.

He keeps lessons.

Skabs is not wise.
He is not clever.
He is not a good judge of complicated things.

He will make mistakes again.

But he will not look away from them.

And when the moment comes—when something stands between harm and those behind him—

Skabs does not step aside.

He plants his feet.
He grips Rûnthar.
And he becomes what he was named to be:

A flawed, stubborn, unyielding warden—

Who stands anyway.
